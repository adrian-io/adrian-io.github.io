My master's thesis studies how training compute should be distributed across the timestep axis of a flow-matching model; my statistical work — energy price forecasting, causal structure learning, crisis early warning — has been about predictive intervals rather than point estimates. The PhD I am looking for sits exactly where those two lines cross: uncertainty quantification for deep generative models, and calibration under distribution shift.

Adaptive timestep sampling for flow matching

Master's thesis at CompVis. Does reinforcement-learned timestep sampling transfer from denoising diffusion to conditional optimal-transport flow matching? Result: no — adaptive sampling matches uniform within run-to-run variation (FID 14.18 vs 13.83) for ~10% more wall-clock, because the loss-reduction reward decays to nothing as the backbone converges. The per-timestep difficulty profile turns out to be U-shaped and stationary, and noise-biased sampling actively hurts sample quality.

About

I came to machine learning through industrial engineering at KIT, and to statistics through a Foreign Office project on predicting armed conflict — which is where I learned that a forecast without an honest interval is a liability rather than an asset.

Since then: a semester of statistics and operations research in Barcelona, an Elite Master's in Data Science at LMU Munich, a thesis in the Computer Vision & Learning group on how flow-matching models should spend their training budget, and four years of applied work — data warehouses at FINN, retrieval-augmented chatbots at Scalable Capital, crisis early warning in Berlin.

What holds it together is a preference for models that are explicit about their own uncertainty. That is the thread I want to keep pulling in a PhD, preferably somewhere the answer matters — energy systems, climate, infrastructure.

Outside that: three and a half languages, long-distance hiking maps, and an ongoing argument with myself about whether Bayesian methods scale.
